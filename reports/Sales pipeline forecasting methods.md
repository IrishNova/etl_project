# Build calibrated, leakage-free pipeline bookings forecasts

A credible new-business revenue forecast is not one model but a four-layer system, and the research converges on a clear build order. First, **stop trusting the CRM's stated stage probability** — it is "usually set once during CRM setup and rarely updated," drifts optimistic, and must be treated as an uncalibrated score to be measured against outcomes and remapped ([mo.agency](https://www.mo.agency/blog/why-your-sales-forecast-is-wrong)). Second, replace point estimates with a **distribution**: weighted pipeline returns only a mean and "literally cannot" answer "what is the chance we miss by $2M," which a Monte Carlo over per-deal win/loss, close-timing, and amount draws can ([Spreadsheet Planet](https://spreadsheetplanet.com/?p=30063)). Third, **blend bottom-up pipeline (reliable only out to about one sales cycle) with a top-down run-rate forecast** using a horizon-dependent weight, because beyond the creation-to-close lead time the pipeline is structurally "unborn" and coverage understates true bookings ([Weflow](https://www.weflow.ai/blog/out-quarter-pipeline-view)). Fourth, **backtest point-in-time**: a live CRM stores only current values, so honest evaluation requires reconstructing "what we knew as of date T" from a stage-change log via an as-of join, walking the origin forward, and scoring with dollar-weighted and proper scoring-rule metrics (WAPE, pinball, CRPS/WIS, PICP, Brier) rather than MAPE ([Coffee.ai](https://www.coffee.ai/articles/accurate-pipeline-forecasting-in-crm); [arXiv:2203.10716](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10716)). The entire stack is implementable in pandas, scikit-learn, lifelines, and numpy. The evidence is strong on mechanism but thin on sales-specific benchmarks — most accuracy numbers are unverified vendor claims, flagged throughout.

## The CRM probability field is an uncalibrated score, so measure it and remap it

The starting estimator is deceptively simple — of deals that reached a stage, what fraction eventually closed won (**16 Closed Won ÷ 40 deals that reached Stage 2 = 40%**) — but it is biased unless you anchor on *when a deal entered the stage* rather than when it closed, measure cohorts/flows rather than period-terminal deals, and treat still-open deals as **right-censored** rather than dropping them ([SaaSGrid](https://saasgrid.com/pipeline/stage-win-rate); [Databox](https://databox.com/?p=189391)). Dropping open deals is a survivorship bias that inflates the rate, because "survival is the opposite of random" ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/blogs/survivorship-bias-in-performance-data/)); a Kellblog worked example showed cohort win rates of **50% narrow / 43% broad before** open deals were allowed to resolve, versus a true **57% narrow / 40% broad after** — the "rolling hairball" problem ([Kellblog](https://www.kellblog.com/how-to-align-marketing-budgets-with-sales-targets-using-win-rates-close-rates-and-milestone-vs-flow-analysis/)). The defensible pandas recipe is to group deals by stage-entry month and segment, compute won ÷ (won + lost) only on *resolved* cohorts, and report the share still open alongside it so censoring stays visible; with many open deals, a Kaplan-Meier curve on time-from-stage-entry recovers an eventual-win estimate from partial histories, and the gap to the naive won÷closed figure quantifies the inflation ([Gelman/statmodeling](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2019/10/08/bizstat-modeling-performance-indicators-for-deals/)). Segment before computing — run new business, expansion, and renewal separately because "mixing them destroys accuracy" — and compute rates both count-weighted and amount-weighted, since amount-weighting "often reveals that early-stage probabilities are inflated by small deals that close easily" ([Databox](https://databox.com/?p=189391); [Prospeo](https://prospeo.io/s/weighted-pipeline)).

When a constant per-stage rate hides systematic variation by deal age, size, product, source, segment, or rep, move to a classifier. **Logistic regression is the standard interpretable, natively-probabilistic starting point, and is what IBM Research deployed** on four years of worldwide data — chosen because it "can directly produce probability instead of a score," is efficient, and is interpretable to business stakeholders ([arXiv:1502.06229](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.06229)). IBM's feature set — geography, binned deal size, sector, new-client flag, lead age (days since created), industry, product — plus a crucial *time-interaction trick* (multiplying week number by static profiles so weekly snapshots of the same open deal are usable rather than redundant replicas) maps directly onto a CRM stage-history table and prevents leaking the eventual outcome across snapshot rows ([arXiv:1502.06229](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.06229)). Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost/LightGBM) "typically deliver the best accuracy for structured sales data" but this rests on vendor guidance, not a head-to-head sales Brier benchmark, which the notes flag as a gap ([Lilly Tech](https://aischool.lillytechsystems.com/ai-deal-scoring/scoring-models.html)). Treat logistic coefficients as odds ratios and *not causally* — "CISO engaged" may reflect deal quality rather than cause a win — and expect non-linearity (an RIT thesis found mid-market highest-probability while very large enterprises carried *negative* SHAP contributions), so bin or spline numeric features ([marini.systems](https://marini.systems/en/glossary/logistic-regression/); [RIT thesis #12707](https://repository.rit.edu/theses/12707)).

Whatever produces the probability, calibration is a separate, non-optional step: a well-calibrated score means that among deals given ~0.8, "approximately 80% actually belong to the positive class" ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)). Diagnose with a reliability curve (`calibration_curve` / `CalibrationDisplay`) paired with a per-bin histogram, and the Brier score — but note Brier is a strictly proper score mixing calibration, resolution, and uncertainty, so "a lower Brier loss does not necessarily mean a better calibrated model"; read it *with* the reliability diagram ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)). Boosted trees and SVMs push probability mass away from 0/1 (sigmoid distortion) and need correction, while logistic regression is often well-calibrated by default ([Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana, ICML 2005](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2005/niculescumizil2005icml-predicting)). Fix with **Platt scaling** (sigmoid; parametric, best for small/under-confident data, strictly monotonic so it preserves AUC) or **isotonic regression** (non-parametric via Pool-Adjacent-Violators, "corrects any monotonic distortion," performs "as well as or better than sigmoid" above ~1000 samples, but overfits on small data and can change AUC) ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html); [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/calibration-isotonic-platt)). The critical anti-leakage rule: fit the calibrator on data disjoint from the classifier's training set, via `CalibratedClassifierCV` (or `FrozenEstimator` + holdout for an already-fitted model) — reusing training outputs "would result in a biased calibrator" ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)). The decisive synthesis is that **the CRM "probability" field is just another uncalibrated score**: bin deals by the CRM's stated value (its natural bins are 10/25/50/75%), plot observed win fraction per bin, and the diagonal gap is the miscalibration; because the distortion is systematically optimistic the curve will sit below the diagonal (stated > actual), and an isotonic fit gives a stated→empirical lookup that compresses the inflated high values downward ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html); [mo.agency](https://www.mo.agency/blog/why-your-sales-forecast-is-wrong)). Detect miscalibration by exporting ≥4 quarters (≥8 if the cycle exceeds 90 days) of closed deals and backtesting quarterly; historical models "require at least 12 to 18 months of clean CRM history to be meaningful" ([coffee.ai](https://www.coffee.ai/articles/sales-pipeline-mistakes-forecasting-accuracy); [TheSalesBlog](https://www.thesalesblog.com/blog/improve-sales-forecasts-with-individually-weighted-pipelines)). The CRM-figure anecdotes (a 25% field where history supported 20%; 68% vs 23% win rates by dwell time) are single-vendor and unverified.

## A three-draw Monte Carlo turns point estimates into a bookings distribution

Weighted pipeline — multiply each open deal's amount by a stage win probability and sum (e.g. **$2,500 + $16,000 + $52,000 = $70,500**) — is cheap and intuitive but structurally limited: a sum of amount×probability returns only the expected value and "cannot express 'there is a 10% chance we miss by $2M,'" exactly what commit/risk decisions need ([Prospeo](https://prospeo.io/s/weighted-pipeline); [SiftHub](https://www.sifthub.io/glossary/weighted-pipeline)). It also averages away rep variation (a 41% blanket negotiation-stage rate hid individuals ranging **12%–75%**) and weights a stalled deal the same as a fast-moving one ([The Sales Blog](https://www.thesalesblog.com/blog/improve-sales-forecasts-with-individually-weighted-pipelines)). The key reframing is that the weighted-pipeline number equals the *mean* of the Monte Carlo distribution when win probabilities are independent and amounts/timing are fixed — so Monte Carlo is a strict superset, and the weighted sum is its degenerate special case.

The full simulation draws three quantities per open deal per trial and repeats N times to build an empirical bookings CDF from which P10/P50/P90 and probability-of-target are read ([Spreadsheet Planet](https://spreadsheetplanet.com/?p=30063); [Ewance](https://ewance.com/en/challenges/sales-forecasting-for-a-saas-scale-up-moving-upmarket-spreadsheet-modeling/)). The per-trial algorithm, assembled from the component sources:

| Draw | What | How (Python) | Source caveat |
|------|------|--------------|---------------|
| 1. Win/loss | `win_i ~ Bernoulli(p_i)` with calibrated `p_i`, optionally shifted by a shared regime factor | numpy; p_i from the calibrated model above | Independent draws understate the tails |
| 2. Timing | if won, sample a close date conditional on the deal's current age | lifelines survival/hazard model, `conditional_after=age` | Timing slippage is often the dominant quarter-level error |
| 3. Amount | sample from a distribution, not the CRM point value | lognormal (keeps revenue positive) or triangular low/expected/high | Large deals often have *lower* win rates, so amount may correlate with win |

Each trial places won revenue into the period containing its sampled close date; stacking N trials per period yields that period's distribution ([Spreadsheet Planet](https://spreadsheetplanet.com/?p=30063); [Ewance](https://ewance.com/en/challenges/sales-forecasting-for-a-saas-scale-up-moving-upmarket-spreadsheet-modeling/); [lifelines docs](https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Survival%20Regression.html)). **Independence is the trap that makes the forecast look falsely precise.** Deals sharing a driver (one macro regime, one rep, one segment) are structurally the correlated-Bernoulli problem from credit-portfolio default modeling, and the naive Gaussian-copula shortcut fails because "setting latent Gaussian correlations equal to target Bernoulli correlations is generally incorrect after thresholding" ([Wong & Ben Chaouch, arXiv:2607.16801](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.16801)). The pragmatic implementation that avoids solving a feasibility LP is a one-factor model: draw a shared scenario factor per trial (a market multiplier `m ~ Normal(1, σ)` or a latent good/bad-quarter state) and shift every deal's win probability before the Bernoulli draw — with σ=0 recovering the artificially narrow independent case, and the loading *calibrated to reproduce an observed cohort-level win-rate variance* rather than set equal to a target pairwise correlation. Correlation fattens both tails without necessarily moving the mean, which is precisely the systematic risk a commit decision cares about.

For the timing draw, survival analysis is the right tool because it keeps open deals as right-censored observations instead of discarding them — averaging days-to-close over only closed deals "will usually under-estimate the expected lifetime of a deal" ([Gelman/statmodeling](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2019/10/08/bizstat-modeling-performance-indicators-for-deals/)). Measure on *tenure* (time since creation), not calendar time. The menu is Kaplan-Meier (non-parametric curve), Cox proportional hazards (regress closing rate on deal size, source, stakeholders), and parametric AFT (Weibull/log-normal) ([InsertChat](https://insertchat.com/glossary/survival-analysis-stats)); **discrete-time hazard is the most natural fit for a period-by-period bookings forecast** because fit as ordinary logistic regression on a deal-period panel (one row per at-risk interval, target 0 until the event period then 1), it directly outputs `h(t) = P(close in period t | still open at t)` — exactly the quantity you multiply through to allocate bookings across future months — and "any binary classification method" (including gradient boosting) can replace the logistic base, with "limited loss in predictive accuracy" versus continuous-time Cox ([bookdown Ch.5](https://bookdown.org/mpfoley1973/survival/discrete.html); [BMC Med Res Methodol 2022](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s12874-022-01679-6)). For an open deal, condition on elapsed age: lifelines' `conditional_after` kwarg gives `S(t)/S(s)` so `predict_median(open_deals, conditional_after=age)` returns *remaining* time (total = prediction + age), which generally pushes the expected close later than the unconditional distribution ([lifelines docs](https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Survival%20Regression.html)). The principled unification of draws 1 and 2 is a **competing-risks / cause-specific hazard** model treating "closes-won in period t" as the event (won vs. lost), since win and timing share drivers — though the notes flag this as a standard-theory inference, not a sales-specific cited source.

Two distinct uncertainties must enter the band. Run count governs only how precisely you resolve *outcome/aleatoric* noise, converging as 1/√N (halving the error takes ~4× the runs), and **tail percentiles are the bottleneck** — estimated 1%/5% percentiles "still fluctuate noticeably at tens of thousands of runs" because a quantile's standard error scales inversely with the outcome density there ([SAS blog](https://blogs.sas.com/content/iml/?p=58261); [UiO STK2510](https://www.uio.no/studier/emner/matnat/math/nedlagte-emner/STK2510/v09/undervisningsmateriale/ch2b.pdf)). Since the decision-relevant numbers are usually P10/P50/P90 rather than extremes, **10k–50k trials generally stabilize those deciles**; validate empirically by running several seeds and checking the P10/P90 spread is within tolerance. The vendor run-count figures (2,137 runs for 3% error; 33,178 for 1%) assume a normal population and a chi-squared test on variance and are strictly for standard deviations, so treat them as order-of-magnitude only. The deeper point is that **parameter/epistemic uncertainty must be added as an outer layer** — win probabilities, hazard coefficients, and amount parameters are themselves estimated from finite data — or coverage falls short, a Monte Carlo result from Pascual, Romo & Ruiz (2001) showing "average coverage... closer to the nominal value when intervals are constructed incorporating parameter uncertainty," with the gap largest for small samples and non-Gaussian errors ([UC3M working paper](https://e-archivo.uc3m.es/bitstream/handle/10016/6527/ws100301.pdf?sequence=1)). The right structure is therefore a **nested two-level Monte Carlo**: the outer loop draws a parameter set (win probabilities from Beta posteriors or logistic-coefficient sampling distributions, hazard coefficients from their covariance, amount parameters from a bootstrap) and the inner loop does the per-deal draws. The data-light way to get this without asymptotic covariance math is a **nonparametric bootstrap** — resample historical deals with replacement, refit the win/hazard/amount models on each resample, run a few inner simulations per resample — which propagates estimation error with no Gaussianity assumption ([arXiv:1804.09302](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.09302); [Ruiz/UBC](https://www.stat.ubc.ca/node/4396)).

## The pipeline is trustworthy only out to one sales cycle, then run-rate takes over

Pipeline coverage — open qualified pipeline ÷ target — is the bridge between a raw pipeline number and expected bookings, and its required level is **the inverse of win rate**: a 33% win rate needs ~3x, 25% needs ~4x, 20% needs ~5x, so the common "3x–4x rule" holds only when win rate matches the ~25–33% it implicitly assumes ([Clari](https://www.clari.com/blog/pipeline-coverage-best-practices/)). Compute required coverage as ≈ (1 / win rate) × buffer, per segment, because win rates differ (SMB ~2.5–3x, enterprise 3–5x or 4–7x at 15–25% win rates) ([fullcast](https://www.fullcast.com/content/sales-coverage-ratios/)). Coverage quality matters as much as quantity — above ~5x can signal too many low-probability deals — and coverage decays within a period from ~3x at the start toward ~1x at the end, so each reading is judged against the expected point in the build, not a flat target ([Clari](https://www.clari.com/blog/pipeline-coverage-best-practices/); [Weflow](https://www.weflow.ai/blog/out-quarter-pipeline-view)). Reading coverage *by target quarter* is how you see trustworthiness decay: a concrete example showed the current quarter healthy at 3.3x against a $32M target while the next quarter sat at 0.4x against $36M — a serious gap visible only in the out-quarter view ([Weflow](https://www.weflow.ai/blog/out-quarter-pipeline-view)). The segment benchmark ranges conflict across vendors and cite no underlying dataset; treat them as directional.

The structural reason the far quarter looks empty is the **creation-to-close lead time**: a pipeline is only "complete" for a target period once enough time has passed for the deals that will close in it to have been created. With a 180-day cycle "everything in the pipeline now had to be created ~2 quarters ago, so the current/next quarter can't be materially changed," and a backward-funnel example traced ~270 days (~9 months) lead-to-close, meaning a lead-volume shortfall surfaces in bookings ~9 months later ([Intelligence by Intent](https://intelligencebyintent.com/insights/the-next-quarter-is-already-baked)). The widely-cited Implisit benchmark puts B2B lead→opportunity at ~84 days and opportunity→close at ~18 days ([Klipfolio](https://www.klipfolio.com/resources/kpi-examples/sales/sales-cycle-length)). The notes' synthesized **empirical detection recipe** for the reliable horizon H: build the distribution of (close date − creation date) from won deals and take a high percentile (P75–P90) as the "fully-born" horizon; for each future period t, the fraction of eventual deals already created ≈ the CDF of that lead-time distribution evaluated at (period start − today); pipeline coverage is trustworthy only where that fraction is high, and the crossover where "% of pipeline already born" drops below an acceptable threshold is the natural handoff to run-rate. Track pipeline by *creation cohort* and compare cohorts only at the same age (young cohorts show lower close rates purely from cycle length), so a cohort tracking below norm at the same age is a quality problem, not a volume gap ([Weflow](https://www.weflow.ai/blog/out-quarter-pipeline-view)). The exact "runs dry" boundary has no cited formal statistical test — the percentile/CDF approach is a synthesized inference.

CRM **forecast categories** are the natural interface for blending because they already separate high-confidence near-term bookings from speculative pipeline: Closed (won), Commit (~90–100%, rep stands behind it), Best Case (plausible, condition-dependent), Pipeline (~10–50%, "shouldn't drive commitments"), and Omitted (excluded) ([Rework](https://resources.rework.com/vi/libraries/pipeline-management/forecast-categories); [Outreach](https://www.outreach.ai/resources/blog/sales-forecast-categories)). They nest cumulatively — in Salesforce, Best Case *includes* Commit and Closed — giving a built-in band (Commit = conservative floor, Best Case = mid, full weighted pipeline = upside). Governance is the risk: Omitted gets abused as a "soft delete" to dodge coverage conversations, and ungoverned Commit "can behave like an optimistic stage field," so hold each category to the same calibration test the win-probability field gets — does "commit" actually close at a high rate ([Outreach](https://www.outreach.ai/resources/blog/sales-forecast-categories); [coffee.ai](https://www.coffee.ai/articles/accurate-pipeline-forecasting-in-crm)).

Best practice for the handoff is **horizon-dependent weighting treated as a blend, not a hard switch**: run bottom-up pipeline and top-down run-rate as two independent forecasts of the same quantity and set weights w(t) so pipeline dominates near-term and decays to ~0 beyond H while run-rate rises to ~1 — using the "% of pipeline already born" CDF from the lead-time analysis as w(t) gives a smooth, data-driven handoff ([Salesmotion](https://salesmotion.io/blog/sales-forecasting-methods)). The academic forecast-combination literature endorses this: combination beats single forecasts with the greatest benefit at short horizons, and **equal-weight averaging is the recommended benchmark, especially under high uncertainty** ([Lawrence, Edmundson & O'Connor 1986, Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.32.12.1521)); judgment integrated with statistics helps when experts have domain knowledge but "HARMS accuracy when judgment is biased or unstructured," and human input works better as an *input* to the statistical process than as after-the-fact adjustment of its output ([Armstrong & Collopy 1998](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v32y1986i12p1521-1532.html)). The rigorous version of "triangulate and reconcile the gap" is hierarchical **forecast reconciliation**: treat period-level pipeline forecasts and the aggregate trend as a hierarchy and reconcile via ỹ = S·P·ŷ, where bottom-up loses no low-level detail but accumulates error upward, top-down is biased when leaf series grow differently, and **MinT** (Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos & Hyndman 2019) minimizes reconciled-error variance with G = (SᵀW⁻¹S)⁻¹SᵀW⁻¹ and "generally performs best" — available in Python via Nixtla's HierarchicalForecast and darts ([arXiv:2510.20383](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.20383); [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/blogs/forecast-reconciliation-top-down-vs-bottom-up/)). **Three double-counting risks must be guarded explicitly**: counting both carry-in pipeline and in-period created-and-closed business in the same target (the explicit Weflow anti-double-count: subtract business you historically create *and* close within the same period); summing nested categories (Best Case already includes Commit/Closed); and adding a bottom-up pipeline number to a run-rate that already reflects the same deals — blend or reconcile, never sum ([Weflow](https://www.weflow.ai/blog/out-quarter-pipeline-view)). The "unborn pipeline" region beyond H is exactly where bottom-up must defer to top-down, filled either by run-rate extrapolation or a pipegen-capacity backward-funnel model tying future bookings to current lead/MQL velocity — though no cited source gives a *validated probabilistic* lead-velocity-to-bookings model (the backward-funnel examples are deterministic point estimates), and no closed-form weighting schedule exists in the literature. The only externally-attributed accuracy figure, Gartner's "fewer than 25% of orgs forecast within 10% of actuals," has an internal inconsistency in its secondary source and should be verified before citing; the ±X% accuracy bands in practitioner articles are unsourced.

## Point-in-time reconstruction and proper scoring rules make the backtest honest

A live CRM "cannot show what the pipeline looked like on a past date" because the opportunity table keeps only current values — so you must persist history as periodic snapshots or a stage-change event log, and **reconstruct "what we knew as of date T" with an as-of join** ([Coffee.ai](https://www.coffee.ai/articles/accurate-pipeline-forecasting-in-crm)). The two canonical patterns are **SCD Type 2** (close the old row, insert a new version; an as-of query filters `valid_from <= T AND valid_to > T` to return exactly the version valid at T) and event sourcing; a stage-change log is an event stream, and an SCD2 table is its materialized projection where each row's `valid_from` is an event time and `valid_to` the next event for the same opportunity, turning reconstruction into a range filter ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/design-implement-data-modeling-unity-catalog/7-implement-slowly-changing-dimension-type-2)). In pandas this is `pd.merge_asof(labels, features, on="ts", by="opp_id", direction="backward")`, the same leakage fix feature stores expose as a point-in-time join ([RisingWave](https://risingwave.com/blog/point-in-time-joins-ml-training-sql); [Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/point-in-time-correctness-in-real-time-machine-learning-32770f322fb1)). Because CRM data is *restated*, **bitemporal modeling** matters: keep both valid time (when it was true in the business) and transaction/record time (when the system learned it), so the label uses final valid-time truth (did the deal book, for how much, in period P) while every feature uses only transaction-time ≤ T knowledge — a close-date or amount corrected after T must not retroactively improve a past forecast. The notes flag that no primary bitemporal reference (e.g. Snodgrass) was located; the framing is synthesized from the as-of-join and SCD2 sources.

The dominant leakage trap is training or evaluating on information absent at the forecast origin: final deal outcomes, fields updated after T, restated close dates/amounts, and preprocessing fit on the full series. **Survivorship is the pipeline-specific analog of look-ahead** — a backtest built from today's CRM only sees opportunities that still exist, so deleted/archived/purged deals and early drop-outs are missing, pushing historical win and conversion rates too high; mitigate by including *every* opportunity open at each snapshot date (including those later lost or removed) and verifying snapshot record counts stay stable, since a sudden drop signals purged rows ([Coffee.ai](https://www.coffee.ai/articles/accurate-pipeline-forecasting-in-crm); [arXiv:2203.10716](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10716)). The practical checklist: freeze features at T via as-of join; derive the label from final outcomes but never expose post-T fields as features; fit scalers/imputers/encoders on the training window only; include churned/deleted opportunities in each snapshot; leave a horizon-length gap between train and target windows; and unit-test the reconstruction by confirming a re-run "as of T" never references a timestamp > T. No source quantifies the *magnitude* of inflation from each trap in a sales setting, so the Coffee.ai figures are indicative only.

Evaluation should use **rolling-origin (walk-forward) backtesting**: move the forecast origin forward through time, always training on the past and testing on the future, giving many forecasts per horizon and mirroring production — never random k-fold, which can put future observations in training ([Hyndman](https://robjhyndman.com/hyndsight/tscv/); [arXiv:2203.10716](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10716)). For bookings the origin is each as-of snapshot date and the horizon is the number of future periods forecast (e.g. end-of-quarter commit from a week-6 snapshot); use an expanding window for short series and a sliding window after regime changes (new pricing, re-org, new product). Crucially, **scikit-learn's `TimeSeriesSplit` adds no gap**, so you must enforce a gap of at least the horizon to prevent target leakage (sktime's `SlidingWindowSplitter` handles this via `window_length`/`fh`/`step_length`), and always benchmark against naïve and seasonal-naïve forecasts ([arXiv:2203.10716](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10716)).

Scoring splits into two complementary layers. For **point accuracy on dollar bookings** — lumpy, a few large deals dominating — prefer **WAPE = Σ|eₜ| / Σ|yₜ|** as the headline KPI (scale-free but not inflated by small values, stable near zero) over MAPE, which is "undefined when yₜ=0," explodes on small denominators (a drop to 20 units can produce a ~6000% APE), and is asymmetric; pair WAPE with signed mean error (chronic over- vs under-commit is the RevOps question) and RMSE if large misses must be penalized hard ([Baeldung](https://www.baeldung.com/cs/mape-vs-wape-vs-wmape); [arXiv:2203.10716](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.10716)). MASE/RMSSE scale by a naïve forecast's in-sample error and are comparable across series — use Hyndman & Koehler (2006) for the canonical MASE formula, which the notes could not capture from a primary source. Because MAE targets the median and RMSE the mean, **match the training loss to the evaluation metric** (training L2 while evaluating MAE is unfair), and report metrics *per horizon* since accuracy should tighten as the period closes. For the **uncertainty band**, use proper scoring rules that reward reliability and sharpness simultaneously, which coverage or width alone cannot: **pinball/quantile loss** (strictly proper, reduces to MAE/2 at τ=0.5), the **interval score** (decomposes into sharpness + over/under-prediction penalties), the **Weighted Interval Score (WIS)** — the pragmatic choice for Monte-Carlo quantile output, built for the COVID-19 Forecast Hub and approximating CRPS for many levels — and **CRPS** for a full predictive distribution ([Bracher, Ray, Gneiting & Reich 2021, PLoS Comp Biol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7880475/); [CRPS Learning, arXiv:2102.00968](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.00968)). Report **PICP (interval coverage) alongside width** so a band is not judged well-calibrated merely for being wide — a well-calibrated 80% interval should contain ~80% of outcomes ([AWS Fortuna](https://aws-fortuna.readthedocs.io/en/stable/references/metric.html)). At the deal level the win-probability model is a binary forecast scored with **Brier score + reliability diagram + ECE**, closing the loop on the miscalibrated-CRM-field problem (the 0.05 "meaningful miscalibration" ECE cutoff is a tool rule of thumb, not a standard). Python implementations are direct: `sklearn.metrics.mean_pinball_loss` and `brier_score_loss`, `sklearn.calibration.calibration_curve`, `properscoring` or `scoringrules` for CRPS/WIS, and coverage as a one-line mean of `(lower <= y) & (y <= upper)` ([arXiv:2102.00968](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.00968)). Formal definitions of proper scoring rules trace to Gneiting & Raftery (2007) and Gneiting & Katzfuss (2014), not fetched here; no worked end-to-end example of probabilistic *sales-pipeline* scoring was found, so the mapping from epidemic/energy forecasting is a (domain-agnostic) inference.

## Conclusion

The research reframes sales forecasting as a measurement discipline rather than a judgment call: every probability in the system — the CRM stage field, a model's `predict_proba`, a "Commit" category — is an uncalibrated score whose reliability curve you can plot and remap, and the same backtesting machinery (reliability diagram, Brier, WIS, PICP) validates all of them at once. The single most leveraged architectural decision is to **carry a distribution end-to-end** — calibrated win probability feeding a nested Monte Carlo over win/timing/amount with a shared regime factor and a bootstrap outer loop — because the band width, not the point estimate, is what a commit decision actually consumes, and independent draws with point-estimate parameters produce a band that is demonstrably too tight.

Two unifying threads deserve emphasis going forward. First, **censoring is the same bug in three places** — survivorship-inflated win rates, naive days-to-close averages, and survivorship in the backtest all stem from treating open or disappeared deals as absent rather than right-censored; the survival-analysis framing (Kaplan-Meier, discrete-time hazard, bitemporal reconstruction) fixes all three coherently. Second, **the creation-to-close lead time is the organizing constant of the whole forecast**: it sets how far the bottom-up pipeline is trustworthy, defines the blend weight to the run-rate model, and dictates the train/target gap in walk-forward evaluation. The honest caveat is that the mechanisms are well-grounded in adjacent literatures (credit-portfolio simulation, survival analysis, epidemic forecast scoring, hierarchical reconciliation) while sales-specific empirical validation is thin — most accuracy numbers are unverified vendor claims, several key primary papers were reached only through secondary summaries, and the reliable-horizon CDF weighting and competing-risks framing are synthesized inferences, not cited methods. Build the system, but treat its own backtest — point-in-time, walk-forward, dollar-weighted, calibration-scored — as the only trustworthy evidence of whether it works.
